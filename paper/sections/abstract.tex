\begin{abstract}
Continual reinforcement learning is meant to preserve earlier tasks, yet
existing methods only approximate that requirement. Regularisation prices
retention with a penalty weight fixed before it is known which tasks will come
under pressure, replay approximates it with a stored sample, and modular methods
buy it with frozen parameters. We state it directly. DUEL requires the deployed
policy to stay within a tolerance of a task-specific specialist on every task
seen so far, and enforces that requirement by primal-dual alternation, so each
task's multiplier is set by the optimisation rather than chosen in advance. The
constraint is one-sided, which leaves the policy free to exceed a specialist
while preventing it from falling behind. DUEL assumes past-task simulators
remain available, stores no trajectories, and costs roughly 1.4 to 1.8 times the
cheapest baseline. It is competitive with the state of the art on the
established benchmark, where per-task output heads and tasks drawn from a single
game can substantially mask interference. Under one shared head across fifty
tasks and three seeds it leads the nearest baseline by 0.126 in average
performance and is the only method evaluated with positive backward transfer,
$+0.358$ against $-0.187$, with 91\% of tasks ending above the level at which
they were first learned against 13\% for the nearest competitor. Across five
unrelated Atari games it is the only method evaluated that both retains the
earlier games and learns the last.
\end{abstract}
